Read this first

This guide condenses a research report on how professional loudspeaker makers (Funktion-One, d&b audiotechnik, L-Acoustics, JBL, Meyer Sound, Martin Audio and others) finish their cabinets, plus the working practice of smaller builders. Most of the sources were read through search-engine excerpts, not full documents. Every key figure in this guide (film thickness, drying time, spray settings, mixing ratio, temperature limits) must be checked against the technical data sheet (TDS) of the actual product you buy. Where the TDS and this guide disagree, the TDS wins. Figures marked ESTIMATE are calculations or shop rules of thumb, not published values.

1Purpose and one-page summary

What we want

A dark grey, matte, textured finish on birch plywood loudspeaker cabinets that survives rental and touring use: truck loading, stacking, dragging on concrete, rain at load-in and hot humid storage. It should scuff rather than chip, hide small scratches in its texture, and be repairable in-house with a roller.

Colour specification

Specification
  • Colour: RAL 7016 Anthracite Grey (primary). Acceptable alternatives: RAL 7021 Black Grey, RAL 7024 Graphite Grey. Do not mix shades within one set of cabinets.
  • Gloss: matt. When ordering tinted paint specify "matt" or "dead matt", gloss 10 GU or less at 60° (ISO 2813). Texture scatters light, so a textured coat reads matte anyway, but a smooth satin repair patch will shine: order matt regardless.
  • Texture and reference: fine to medium "orange peel" spatter, even and identical across a set. Keep a physical RAL 7016 card in the shop and approve the first test panel against it in daylight.
Warning: grey chalks in sunlight on the wrong chemistry

Standard polyurea and PU bed-liners use aromatic isocyanates, which fade and chalk under UV. Barely visible on black, on grey it shows as a pale, dusty surface within months outdoors. For cabinets that see sun, topcoat any aromatic polyurea or PU (Methods B and C) with a pigmented aliphatic PU or water-based acrylic topcoat in RAL 7016 matt. Method A acrylics are UV stable and need no topcoat.

Non-negotiable rules

  1. Water-based texture paint goes on bare, sanded wood only, or on the same water-based paint. Never over old PU, polyurea, solvent, cellulose or 2K paint, primer or undercoat: it flakes.
  2. Never thin coats. Three to four full coats (or two heavy spray passes to about 120 µm dry). "Thin light coats won't do the job."
  3. Round every outside edge to 6 to 13 mm (1/4 to 1/2 in) radius before painting. Sharp edges chip first.
  4. Seal filler patches and screw-hole filler before painting. Do not prime whole panels for Method A.
  5. Work at 12 to 25 °C (54 to 77 °F), moderate humidity. Hot climate: shade, surface below 30 °C, air movement.
  6. No stacking, packing or hardware fitting before 24 hours. No touring before full cure, 4 to 7 days.
  7. One test panel per batch: same ply, prep, paint and day. Label it, keep it, adhesion-test it at 7 days.
  8. Isocyanate products (Methods B and C) only with supplied-air breathing apparatus, extraction and trained staff; nobody else in the room.
  9. Previously painted or fiberglass cabinets: see Section 5A. Identify the old paint, 2K epoxy primer mandatory, test area before the batch.

Why a thick, textured, elastic coating resists damage

  • Thickness times toughness, not hardness. A 1 to 1.5 mm polyurea skin (Shore D about 55, elongation 80 to 200 percent on published liner data sheets) spreads a point load and springs back. A 0.12 mm acrylic film has far less material to lose before wood shows. This is why polyurea is the top of every durability ranking.
  • Hard thin films crack on wood. Plywood moves with humidity. Epoxies, 2K clearcoats and powder-type films are hard but brittle. JBL explicitly warns not to use epoxy paints on its wooden cabinets because they crack.
  • Texture hides damage. A matte spatter texture conceals fine scratches and makes scuffs less visible. It also lets a roller repair blend in. Every brand uses texture for this reason, not only for looks.
  • Sealed substrate. Touring brands use exterior or marine birch with D4 glue, coat inside and out on weather versions, and treat raw wood before assembly (Meyer). A coating cannot protect plywood that swells under it.
  • Edges and cases. No touring brand publishes an edge radius. They rely on the elastomer wrapping the edge, recessed handles, and flight cases or padded covers for transport. For a thin acrylic coat, the edge radius matters much more.

Important gap

No loudspeaker maker and no water-based paint maker publishes scratch, abrasion (Taber), hardness (Shore, pencil) or adhesion (cross-hatch) figures for cabinet coatings. Tip: choosing within Method A

Prefer a product the maker sells as a loudspeaker cabinet paint and that states "no primer on bare wood" (Warnex, Tuff Cab, DuraTex, Armacab). Tuff Cab Pro (satin-matt) is closer to Warnex than Tuff Cab Standard (gloss). If only a general-purpose local acrylic texture paint is available, it may still work, but you must prove adhesion and hardness on an offcut first (Section 8), because its adhesion to bare ply and its hardness are unknown.

3.3 Hot, humid climate notes (general)

The data sheets quote drying times at 20 °C and 65 % RH. At 28 to 34 °C and 70 to 90 % RH the behaviour changes. Section 12.3 gives the Indonesia-specific limits and supplier details; the general rules are:

  • Water-based paint (A): water leaves the film slowly when the air is already saturated. Expect dust-dry and recoat times two to three times longer ESTIMATE. At the same time, a hot surface above about 30 °C skins over before the film underneath is dry, which traps water and weakens the coat. Paint in shade or indoors, never in direct sun, keep air moving with fans (not aimed at wet paint), and run a dehumidifier in the drying room if you have one. Allow 48 h before stacking and 7 days before touring. Store paint at 15 to 25 °C, never in the sun, never frozen.
  • 2K PU (B): pot life halves roughly for every 10 °C rise ESTIMATE, so 60 min at 20 °C becomes about 30 min at 30 °C. Isocyanate reacts with moisture, which can cause pinholes and bubbles in humid air. Mix small quantities, spray in the coolest part of the day, keep the cabinet and the paint at room temperature before use.
  • Polyurea (C): the reaction is so fast that humidity matters less, but moisture in the plywood will cause blisters and poor adhesion. Deliver cabinets dry (wood moisture below about 12 % by meter ESTIMATE) and stored indoors.
  • All methods: plywood takes up moisture in humid storage and swells. Seal end grain and filler, and do not paint cabinets that have been standing in a damp or open store.

4Method A step by step: 1K water-based acrylic texture paint

Primary procedure for an ordinary paint shop. Products of the Warnex, Tuff Cab Pro, DuraTex or Armacab class. Read the product TDS before the first job and write the product's own figures into the quick reference card (Section 11).

Rule

Water-based texture paint bonds to bare, sanded, dust-free wood. It does not bond reliably to primers, acrylic undercoats, filler, glue squeeze-out, wax, old solvent paint, PU or polyurea. Every preparation step below exists to give the paint bare keyed wood, and only bare keyed wood.

Stage 1: Inspection and stripping

1

Inspect the cabinet

  • Check the plywood is dry and stored indoors. If you have a pin moisture meter, aim for below 12 % ESTIMATE. Reject cabinets with delaminated plies, soft or swollen panels, or open joints: repair the woodwork first.
  • Look for glue squeeze-out along joints, wax or oil marks, pencil, and screw heads standing proud. Note voids and chipped plies on edges. All of these must go.
  • Decide: new bare plywood (go to Step 3) or previously painted (Step 2).
2

Previously painted cabinets: identify the old finish and strip if needed

Shop test for the old paint ESTIMATE: on a hidden spot (inside a handle pocket, under the connector plate) rub firmly for 30 seconds with a rag wetted with isopropyl alcohol or methylated spirits. A water-based acrylic texture paint softens and transfers colour onto the rag. A cured 2K PU, polyurea or solvent paint does not move. Gloss, a smooth hard shell, or a "rubbery" 1 mm skin also mean a non-acrylic finish.

  • Old finish is sound water-based texture paint of the same class: wash with warm water and a little detergent, rinse, let dry fully. Scuff the whole surface with P80 to remove gloss and loose paint. Feather chipped areas to bare wood. Go to Step 5 (filling). No stripping needed. This is what Turbosound's manual describes: "paint may be applied successfully on top of existing coats".
  • Old finish is PU, polyurea, solvent, cellulose, 2K, unknown, or flaking: strip to bare wood. Use a belt sander with P40 to P60, then P80. On thick elastomer skins use a heat gun and scraper first, then sand. Do not sand through the face veneer; stop when wood shows. Chemical strippers are reported as "completely useless" on these finishes and leave residues: avoid them.
  • If in doubt after stripping, paint an offcut or one hidden panel, cure 7 days and run the adhesion test (Section 8) before committing the whole set.
  • Fiberglass or fiberglass-skinned cabinets: Method A does not apply. Use Section 5A (2K epoxy primer plus 2K PU).
3

Remove hardware and mask

  • Remove handles, corners, feet, rigging hardware, pole cups, connector plates, grilles, badges and drivers. Bag and label the hardware per cabinet.
  • Plug every threaded insert and screw hole with a short screw, a plastic plug or a tight wad of masking tape. Paint in a thread will stop the screw.
  • Close driver cut-outs from inside with card or a blanking board taped from the inside, so the cut-out edge gets painted but no paint goes inside the box.
  • Mask gasket faces and any machined mating faces (connector plate rebate, amplifier module rebate) that need a flat seal. Use a good quality masking tape and press the edges down; texture paint is thick and creeps under loose tape.
  • Mask the back of the baffle only if the driver gasket needs bare wood. In most cabinets the baffle face is painted.

Stage 2: Woodwork preparation

4

Round the edges: 6 to 13 mm radius

  • Route all outside cabinet edges with a round-over bit: 13 mm (1/2 in) radius on the main cabinet edges, 6 mm (1/4 in) around front and back frames where a larger radius would cut into rebates. These are the values a loudspeaker kit supplier publishes for textured black finishes; no touring manufacturer publishes a figure. The 90-degree edges are "the most vulnerable, where paint seemed to chip most easily".
  • Use a sharp bit and a steady feed; birch ply chips on round-overs if the bit is dull or the ply has voids. Fill any voids exposed by routing (Step 5).
  • Where a router cannot reach, hand-sand to at least a 3 mm radius ESTIMATE. Never leave a knife edge.
  • Rounding also hides butt joints, which "tend to crack paint finishes".
5

Fill voids, screw holes and chipped edges

  • Fill ply voids, countersunk screw heads, chipped edge plies and open joints with a two-part polyester or epoxy wood filler. Overfill slightly, let it cure fully, sand flush.
  • Texture paint hides sanding scratches but does not hide holes, steps or dents. A bed-liner (Method B or C) "shows every imperfection". Fill properly now.
  • Remove all glue squeeze-out with a sharp chisel and sandpaper. Warnex-class paint "does not hold on glue".
6

Sand: P80 then P120, no finer than about P150 to 180

  • Sand all surfaces with P80, then P120, with a belt or orbital sander. Sand edges and end grain by hand along the grain.
  • Do not go finer than P150 to P180. The paint needs a mechanical key. Factory practice is P80 to P120, and the thick paint hides the scratches. German users go as coarse as P60 to P80 for Warnex.
  • MDF parts: sand off the dense, waxy surface skin or the paint will not grip.
  • Any oily hand prints: wipe with water and a little detergent, rinse, dry. No solvent wipe is needed on bare wood.
7

Remove all dust

  • Vacuum every surface, including inside handle pockets and cut-outs. Blow out with compressed air away from the spray area, then vacuum again.
  • Wipe with a lint-free cloth lightly damp with clean water and let the wood dry for 30 minutes. Do not use oily tack cloths or solvent wipes before water-based paint.
  • Dust under the first coat is the most common cause of patchy adhesion. Paint within a few hours of sanding, in a clean area.
8

Seal filler patches only

  • Brush thinned PVA wood glue (about 1 part PVA to 1 part water) or thinned paint over every filler patch and filled screw hole only. Let it dry, then knock back with P120 so it is not glossy. Solvent-based filler in particular must be sealed or it shows as a dull, poorly adhered patch.
  • Optional: a thin wipe of the same thinned PVA on exposed end-grain plywood edges to even out absorption. Keep it thin and sand it; a thick PVA film is itself a poor base for paint.
  • Do not prime or PVA-seal whole panels. Warnex, Tuff Cab and DuraTex are specified to go on bare birch ply or MDF with no primer. Warnex is reported to hold poorly on primers and acrylics.
  • If you are using a different local acrylic texture paint, paint an offcut with and without a thinned first coat, cure 7 days, and run the adhesion test (Section 8) before deciding whether any sealer is needed.

Stage 3: Painting

Warning: temperature and humidity

Work at 12 to 25 °C (54 to 77 °F) air and surface temperature. Below 12 °C the paint dries very slowly and may not film properly. Above 25 °C the surface skins before the film has levelled. In a hot climate: paint in shade or indoors, never in direct sun, keep the cabinet surface below 30 °C (touch test: it should feel cool, not warm), keep air moving, and expect every drying time to be longer in high humidity. Bring paint and cabinets to room temperature the night before. Paint that has frozen is ruined and will never bond.

9

Test panel (mandatory) and texture trial

  • Prepare an offcut of the same plywood, at least 300 x 300 mm (12 x 12 in), with one routed edge, one filled screw hole, sanded P80 to P120 exactly like the cabinets.
  • Paint it first, with every coat, at the same time as the cabinets. Write on the back: date, product, batch number, number of coats, method (roller or gun and settings), temperature.
  • Use it to set the texture: adjust roller pressure or gun settings on the panel until the texture matches the approved sample. Compare colour with the RAL 7016 card in daylight.
  • Keep the panel. Test it at 7 days (Section 8). Keep one approved panel as the reference for texture and colour for all future batches.
10

First coat: thinned sealer coat, if the TDS permits

  • The first coat soaks into the wood and evens out absorption. Apply it with a brush or short-pile roller, working it into end grain, edges and handle pockets.
  • Tuff Cab: the maker allows the first coat thinned 50:50 with water. DuraTex Roller Grade: do not dilute; use Spray Grade if a thinner product is needed. Warnex: thin only with deionised water and only if your TDS permits; for spraying, users insist "do not thin". Follow your TDS.
  • Let the first coat dry until it is dry to the touch and no longer cool: 30 to 60 minutes at 20 °C, 2 to 4 hours in a cool or humid room.
  • Do not sand between coats of the same paint (DuraTex and Warnex: no intermediate sanding). Only knock off dust nibs with a light hand pass of P180 if needed.
11

Texture coats: two to three further coats

Roller method (suits any shop, best for small batches and repairs):

  • Use a honeycomb or coarse open-cell foam texture roller, 100 mm (4 in) for edges and small panels, 180 to 230 mm for large panels. A plain foam roller gives "sand in the paint"; a long-pile roller gives a soft stipple. Test on the panel.
  • Load the roller heavily. Roll in two or three crossing directions, wait about 30 seconds, then roll once lightly in one direction to flatten air bubbles and even the texture.
  • Paint one face at a time, edges first, then the face. Keep a wet edge. Suspend or support the cabinet so you can paint the bottom and feet area; paint the bottom first, then turn.
  • Wrap the roller in a plastic bag between coats so it does not dry out.

Spray method (factory texture, faster for batches):

  • Use a pressure pot or pressure-cup gun with a 1.5 to 2.0 mm nozzle (or an underbody/Schutz-type gun), spraying from 30 to 40 cm (12 to 16 in). A gravity gun needs a 2 mm or larger set-up and gives little texture. A drywall hopper gun with 4 to 6 mm nozzle at low pressure works for DuraTex Spray Grade. Set pot and atomising pressure per the TDS; start low and raise until the paint breaks into even droplets.
  • Keep the system closed: the paint skins quickly in an open pot and the skin blocks the nozzle.
  • The factory "dimple" texture is two passes 15 to 20 minutes apart: first an even wetter pass for coverage, then a drier "spatter" pass with lower air pressure or higher fluid flow to throw larger droplets. Spray the second pass from the same distance, moving steadily; dwell makes blotches.
  • Do not thin to make a small gun work. A user who added 25 % water got endless drying and low film build.

Both methods: wait until each coat is dry to the touch before the next (dust-dry 15 to 20 minutes; recoat typically 1 to 3 hours depending on temperature and humidity). Apply two or three texture coats after the sealer coat until the texture is dense and no wood grain shows through on close inspection.

12

Extra coat on edges and corners

After the last full coat, go round every outside edge, corner and handle-pocket lip with a loaded 100 mm texture roller or a stipple brush and lay one more coat on the edges only. Edges always end up thinner than faces, and they take the hits. Blend the stipple 30 to 50 mm onto the face so there is no visible line.

13

Check film build and consumption

  • Coverage for a rough texture is roughly 2 to 3 m² per kg per coat (Tuff Cab); a French Warnex distributor quotes about 1.5 m²/kg. Plan on 2 m²/kg.
  • Weigh the tin before and after the job. A 12-inch two-way cabinet of about 1.5 to 2 m² outside area should use roughly 2 to 2.5 kg of paint in total ESTIMATE. If you used half that, the coats were too thin: add another.
  • A wet-film comb can be used on a smooth spot (for example inside a handle pocket) to confirm the per-coat wet film the TDS quotes.

5Method B step by step: 2K polyurethane textured coating

For a shop that already sprays two-pack paint safely: booth with extraction, supplied-air respirators, trained staff. Products of the U-POL Raptor class (tintable 2K urethane bed-liner) or a 2K PU texture topcoat from an industrial paint supplier.

Warning: isocyanates

All 2K polyurethane hardeners contain isocyanates, a potent cause of occupational asthma. Spraying produces a mist that a cartridge respirator does not reliably stop. For spraying: supplied-air (airline) breathing apparatus for everyone in the booth, during spraying and during the booth clearance time. A cartridge respirator is acceptable only for rolling or brushing. Nitrile gloves, coveralls with hood, eye protection. Extraction running. Nobody else in the room. Measure and post the booth clearance time. In the EU and Northern Ireland, since 24 August 2023, REACH (Regulation (EU) 2020/1149) requires documented training before professional use of products with more than 0.1 % diisocyanates; UK HSE additionally expects health surveillance for sprayers. Follow your local rules.

Adhesion rules for Method B

Rule
  • Never apply water-based texture paint over 2K PU or polyurea. It will flake. Tuff Cab over polyurethane "will never bond properly".
  • Never apply 2K PU over uncured or old water-based texture paint. Raptor "won't work very well over Tuff Cab". Strip water-based paint to bare wood first.
  • Never apply the liner over bare, unsealed plywood. Reports of bed-liner that "acts as a kind of shell and can start to separate" come from unsealed, unkeyed cabinets. Seal first.
  • Never apply the liner wet-on-wet over fresh primer unless the TDS allows it. Let the sealer cure and sand it for a key.
1

Woodwork preparation

Carry out Section 4, Steps 1 to 7: inspect, strip if needed, remove and mask hardware, round edges 6 to 13 mm, fill, sand P80 to P120, remove dust. Fill more carefully than for Method A: a bed-liner "will show every imperfection", including screw holes.

2

Seal the plywood with a 2K PU or epoxy sealer

  • Apply one or two coats of a 2K polyurethane sealer/primer or a 2K epoxy primer to all surfaces and edges, per its TDS. Builders who seal "with a polyurethane 2-component paint before Line-X" report the best results; an epoxy sealer between fresh paint and the liner "prevents lifting".
  • Let the sealer cure per TDS (usually overnight), then sand with P180 to P240 to a uniform matt key and remove dust. Recoat within the sealer's maximum recoat window; if you miss it, sand again.
  • Do not use a 1K water-based primer under 2K PU.
  • Previously painted cabinets with a sound 2K or PU finish: degrease, sand P120 to P180 to matt, remove dust, and apply the liner. No sealer needed unless bare wood shows; spot-prime bare patches. For unknown old paint and for fiberglass cabinets, follow Section 5A (epoxy primer mandatory).
3

Tint to dark grey

  • Use the tintable version of the liner. Have your paint supplier mix a compatible solvent-based tinter or basecoat to RAL 7016 and add it at the ratio the liner TDS allows (commonly up to about 10 % of the base ESTIMATE, check TDS). Or order the liner factory-tinted to RAL 7016 if the supplier offers it.
  • Ask for matt. Textured liners dry to a low sheen; if the test panel is too glossy, use a matting additive approved by the maker or plan a matt topcoat (Step 7).
  • Spray a test card, let it dry, compare with the RAL 7016 card in daylight before mixing the batch.
4

Mix

  • Raptor class: 3 parts base to 1 part hardener by volume. Add tinter, close, shake or stir for two minutes. Use only the hardener supplied for the product.
  • Pot life 60 minutes at 20 °C. At 30 °C expect about half that ESTIMATE. Mix only what you will spray in 30 minutes.
  • Keep cabinets, base and hardener at room temperature; cold material sprays coarse, hot material gels in the gun.
5

Spray two coats

  • Use a Schutz (underbody) gun or the maker's liner gun on a compressor with a regulator. Air pressure controls texture: higher pressure gives a finer texture, lower pressure a coarser one. Set it on the test panel first; typical range 2.5 to 4 bar ESTIMATE, check TDS.
  • Spray from about 40 to 50 cm, steady passes, 50 % overlap. Edges and corners first, then faces. Do not dwell: the material builds fast and sags.
  • Let the first coat become tacky-dry (typically 30 to 60 minutes at 20 °C, check TDS), then apply the second coat. Two coats normally reach several hundred micrometres dry ESTIMATE; confirm the TDS figure and check the panel with a thickness gauge if you have one.
  • Overspray is sticky and stains anything it lands on. Mask the booth and everything in it.
6

Cure

  • Touch dry in 20 to 30 minutes. Handle after 24 hours. Full hardness about 7 days. Refit hardware after 24 to 48 hours; tour after 7 days.
  • Do not stack face to face before 48 hours. Ventilate the drying area; the smell is solvent and residual isocyanate.
  • Clean guns immediately with the solvent the TDS names. Cured material does not dissolve.
7

UV topcoat for outdoor cabinets

Ask the supplier whether the liner is aromatic or aliphatic. Most bed-liner products are aromatic and will chalk and lighten in sunlight, which is very visible on grey. For cabinets used outdoors, apply a pigmented aliphatic 2K PU topcoat in RAL 7016 matt (or a water-based acrylic topcoat the liner maker approves) after the liner has cured per its recoat window. Sand lightly or follow the TDS for recoat adhesion. Indoor-only cabinets can skip the topcoat.

8

Test panel and inspection

Same as Method A, Steps 9 and 15: a test panel through every stage including sealer, labelled and kept; adhesion and scuff test at 7 days (Section 8). Inspect for colour, even texture, full edge coverage, no sags, no pinholes (a sign of moisture or too-thick wet coats), no paint in threads.

5ARepainting: previously painted and fiberglass cabinets

For the owner's actual stock: batch 1 is plywood cabinets previously painted with an unknown paint, batch 2 is cabinets that appear to be fiberglass (GRP) or fiberglass-skinned plywood, also previously painted. Both batches have already been hand-sanded. The chosen system is Method B / Indonesia Route 2: a 2K epoxy isolating primer, then Jotun Hardtop XP tinted RAL 7016 matt applied with texture (alternative topcoat: Propan DURATEX DRX 890 WB). Everything in Section 5 (mixing, spraying, cure, isocyanate PPE) still applies; this section covers only what is different about old paint and fiberglass.

Why 2K PU over a 2K epoxy primer, and not a water-based texture paint

  • Neither substrate is bare timber. Tuff Cab, Warnex and DuraTex are specified for bare, sanded wood; their makers say they bond poorly over existing finishes, and Tuff Cab "will never bond properly" over PU. Gelcoat and old paint are exactly the surfaces they fail on. Method A is therefore not available here.
  • A 2K epoxy primer is an isolating layer. It bonds mechanically to a keyed, non-porous surface (gelcoat, cured old paint, glass fibre), it is not re-dissolved by the solvent in the 2K topcoat, and it seals the old paint so that the topcoat's thinner does not reach it and lift it. The topcoat then bonds to fresh epoxy, not to an unknown old film.
  • 2K PU is flexible enough for both. A fiberglass skin and a plywood panel both move; an aliphatic 2K acrylic PU such as Hardtop XP is formulated for steel and GRP in marine use and tolerates that movement, while remaining hard, UV stable and tintable to RAL 7016.
  • The epoxy primer is the step that cannot be skipped. Solvent 2K topcoat sprayed straight onto an unidentified old paint can wrinkle it; water-based 1K sprayed onto gelcoat peels in sheets. The primer is cheap insurance against both.
Warning
  • Never spray solvent 2K directly onto an unidentified old paint. Identify it (Step 1), isolate it with epoxy primer, and prove it on a test area first.
  • Never use water-based 1K texture paint over fiberglass or gelcoat. It will not bond.
  • Never leave glossy spots. Any shine left after sanding is an unkeyed surface and will be the first place the new coating lifts.

Procedure

1

Identify the old paint: 30-second solvent rub test

  • On each batch, pick a hidden spot (back panel, under a handle, inside a pole cup). Wet a white rag with nitro thinner / lacquer thinner (cellulose thinner) and rub firmly for 30 seconds.
  • Hard and unaffected (no softening, no colour on the rag): the old paint is a cured 2K PU, polyurea or epoxy. It can be overcoated after keying (Step 2). Epoxy primer is still applied as the standard system.
  • Softens, wrinkles, goes tacky or transfers colour: the old paint is a 1K acrylic, nitro (cellulose) or similar solvent-sensitive film. Remove it wherever it is loose or thin; where it is sound and well bonded, isolate it with the epoxy primer: first pass a light mist coat, flash 10 to 15 minutes, then a full coat. The mist coat limits how much solvent reaches the old paint at once.
  • If the epoxy primer still lifts or wrinkles the old paint on the test area (Step 6), strip that batch to wood (plywood) or to gelcoat (fiberglass) and start again.
  • Record the result per batch on the job sheet. Do not assume both batches carry the same paint.
2

Prepare the old paint (both batches)

  • Remove all loose, flaking, cracked or blistered paint with a scraper and sander back to a firmly bonded edge. Feather every edge with P120 so no step can be felt with a fingertip.
  • Sand all remaining old paint with P120 to P180 to a full, even matt. The owner's hand sanding is the start, not the finish: check every face in raking light and sand again until no gloss remains anywhere, including inside handle pockets, around hardware recesses and along edges.
  • Fill chips, screw holes and damaged corners with 2K filler (polyester or epoxy), cure, sand flush, feather.
  • Accept that the old texture stays. Do not try to sand a textured old finish smooth; the new textured topcoat goes over it and hides it. Only knock down loose peaks and sharp edges.
  • Blow off and vacuum. Degrease with silicone remover or IPA on a clean cloth immediately before priming, wiping in one direction and turning the cloth often. Prime within a few hours of degreasing; do not touch the surface with bare hands afterwards.
3

Fiberglass (GRP) and fiberglass-skinned cabinets: extra steps

  • Epoxy primer is mandatory on every area of exposed gelcoat or glass. Gelcoat is smooth, non-porous and may still carry mould-release wax; nothing else bonds to it reliably.
  • Double degrease with silicone remover before sanding and again after sanding (mould release, wax and polish migrate into the sanding dust and back onto the surface).
  • Sand P120 to P180 to full matt. Where glass fibres are exposed (sanded-through gelcoat, damaged corners), seal them with two coats of epoxy primer, the first worked in with a brush, before any filler; otherwise the fibres wick moisture and the filler prints through.
  • Fill pinholes and voids in the gelcoat with epoxy filler or a fine polyester filler, cure, sand. Pinholes left open become bubbles in the topcoat.
  • Check for cracks along edges and corners. On a fiberglass skin over plywood, a crack usually means the plywood underneath has swelled with moisture and split the skin (the mechanism Fulcrum describes for glass over plywood). Open the crack with a rotary tool or a knife to sound material, let the wood dry (days, in a dry room), fill with epoxy filler, sand, and prime. Do not bridge a crack with paint; it will reopen.
  • Tap every panel: a dull, hollow sound means the skin has separated from the wood. Inject or fill with epoxy and clamp, or reject the cabinet.
4

Prime with 2K epoxy

  • Product: Nippon nax 2200 2K epoxy primer (automotive, 1 L set with hardener, Tokopedia) or Jotun Penguard epoxy primer (industrial, Jotun dealers). Mix per TDS; respect pot life (shorter in heat).
  • Over solvent-sensitive old paint: mist coat, flash 10 to 15 min, then full coat (Step 1). Over hard old paint and gelcoat: one full coat; two coats on exposed glass and bare wood.
  • Let the primer cure per TDS (usually overnight), then sand P240 to a uniform matt, degrease, and apply the topcoat within the primer's recoat window. If the window is missed, sand again.
  • Isocyanate and solvent PPE as Section 5: supplied-air respirator for spraying, extraction, nobody else in the room.
5

Topcoat: Hardtop XP RAL 7016 matt, textured

  • Jotun Hardtop XP tinted to RAL 7016 at an MCI dealer, matt variant or dealer-approved matting (Section 12.2, Route 2). Alternative: Propan DURATEX DRX 890 WB, low sheen, tinted to RAL 7016.
  • First coat heavy through a Schutz or hopper gun to build the texture over the old texture; then one or two normal coats for colour and even sheen. Set the texture on the test area first.
  • Cure as Section 5, Step 6: 24 h handling, 48 h stacking, 7 days before touring; keep RH below 85 % until walk-on dry.
6

Two mandatory test areas before the batch

  • Make one test area on each substrate type: one painted-plywood cabinet and one fiberglass cabinet, in a hidden zone (back panel, under a handle, behind the connector plate), about 150 x 150 mm each, through the full system: prep, degrease, epoxy primer (mist plus full coat where the old paint is solvent-sensitive), sand, topcoat.
  • Watch the primer for 30 minutes after application: any wrinkling, lifting or bubbling of the old paint is a fail.
  • After 48 hours, run the cross-hatch tape test (Section 8, Test 1: X-cut or 2 mm lattice, tape, pull at 180 degrees). Pass is 4B/4A or better. Repeat at 7 days if time allows, and do the 24 h wet soak on the fiberglass area.
  • Fail = strip that batch fully (plywood to wood, fiberglass to sound gelcoat) and re-test. Do not proceed on a marginal result; a 3B on old paint means sheets will come off in the truck.
  • Photograph and label the test areas; keep the results with the job sheet.

Butt joints under the paint also move and "tend to crack paint finishes". Every method in this guide therefore starts with the same geometry work.

8Quality control checklist and in-house tests

No manufacturer publishes abrasion or adhesion figures for these coatings. The shop's own test panel is the only durability data. Run these checks on every batch and keep the records with the job.

Before painting

  • TDS of the exact product read; figures copied into the reference card.
  • Paint batch within shelf life, stored 15 to 25 °C, never frozen.
  • Old finish identified; stripped if not water-based (Method A) or not 2K (Method B).
  • Edges rounded 6 to 13 mm.
  • Filler cured, flush, sealed with thinned PVA or paint.
  • Sanded P80 to P120; no glue, wax or pencil; dust removed.
  • Hardware off, threads plugged, cut-outs blanked, gasket faces masked.
  • Air and surface 12 to 25 °C; shade; RH noted.
  • Test panel prepared from the same ply.

During painting

  • Texture and colour approved on the test panel before the first cabinet.
  • First coat worked into edges and end grain.
  • Each coat dry to the touch before the next; no recoating over a cool, damp film.
  • Two or three texture coats; no visible grain.
  • Extra coat on all edges.
  • Paint weight used per cabinet recorded (target 2 to 2.5 kg per 12-inch top ESTIMATE).
  • Closed pot kept closed; gun cleaned with water promptly (Method A).
  • Method B: pot life respected, PPE and extraction in use, clearance time observed.

After painting

  • 24 h: dry, not tacky, thumb press leaves no mark.
  • Colour and texture match the reference panel in daylight.
  • Edges fully covered; no shiny or thin edges.
  • Masking removed cleanly; threads clear; no paint inside.
  • Hardware refitted after 24 to 48 h with correct screws.
  • 7 days: adhesion and scuff test on the panel passed and recorded.
  • Cabinets stored on battens, separated, 7 days before touring.
  • Job sheet filed: product, batch, coats, weights, temperatures, test results.

Test 1: Cross-hatch adhesion test (ASTM D3359 style)

Do this on the test panel after 7 days of cure, on a face and across the routed edge if possible. On a previously painted cabinet, do it on the test panel made from the stripped cabinet, or in a hidden spot.

  1. With a sharp blade and a steel rule, cut six parallel cuts about 20 mm long through the coating to the wood, 2 mm apart (the spacing for films of 50 to 125 µm). Cut six more at 90 degrees to make a lattice. For thick coatings (Method B and C), make a single X-cut instead: two 40 mm cuts crossing at about 30 to 45 degrees.
  2. Brush the lattice lightly to remove loose flakes.
  3. Apply a strip of strong pressure-sensitive tape (a semi-transparent 25 mm filament or office tape of the type the standard specifies, not masking tape) over the cuts, press down firmly with a fingertip for 90 seconds and rub with a pencil eraser so it grips the texture.
  4. Within 90 seconds, pull the tape off in one smooth motion at close to 180 degrees.
  5. Rate the lattice: 5B no coating removed; 4B small flakes at the intersections, less than 5 % of the area; 3B flaking along edges and at intersections, 5 to 15 %; 2B 15 to 35 %; 1B 35 to 65 %; 0B worse. For the X-cut, 5A no peeling, 4A trace peeling at the intersection, 3A jagged removal along the cuts up to 1.6 mm, lower grades for more.
Acceptance

Pass: 4B or 5B (4A or 5A for the X-cut). 3B is marginal: check preparation and recoat the batch's edges. 2B or worse: the coating is not bonded; find the cause (old paint under it, dust, primer, glue, frozen paint, too cold) and strip and redo. Texture makes rating harder: judge how much of the lattice squares lifted, not whether the tape pulled off texture peaks.

Test 2: Wet adhesion (humid-climate check)

Stand one corner of the test panel in 20 mm of clean water for 24 hours, dry the surface, and repeat the cross-hatch test on the soaked area within 10 minutes. A drop of more than one grade compared with the dry area means the paint, the sealer or the plywood edge sealing is not suitable for humid or outdoor use.

Test 3: Scuff and chip test (in-house, not a standard)

Use the same method every time so results are comparable between products and batches. ESTIMATE

  1. Scuff: wrap a 1 kg weight (a flat steel block) in a fresh grey non-woven abrasive pad (Scotch-Brite type) and drag it 20 strokes of 150 mm over the panel under its own weight. Record: texture intact, texture polished flat, or wood showing. Then do the same with a strip of P120 paper. A good acrylic texture coat polishes but does not show wood after 20 strokes with the pad; 2K PU and polyurea show less change.
  2. Edge chip: drop a 500 g steel ball or a 500 g hammer head from 500 mm onto the routed edge of the panel, three times at different spots. Record: dent only, crack, or chip with wood exposed. Compare against the kept reference panel.
  3. Coin scratch: drag the edge of a coin across the face with firm hand pressure. A pass is a grey mark that wipes off or a flattened texture, not a cut to wood.

Write the results on the back of the panel. When trying a new product or a new local supplier, run Tests 1 to 3 on panels of the current product and the candidate side by side before changing.

9Repair and touch-up procedure

Method A cabinets (water-based texture paint)

1

Clean and sand the damage

Wash the area with water and a little detergent, rinse, dry. Sand the chip and 20 to 30 mm around it with P80, feathering the edges of the old paint so there is no step. Do not sand finer than P80: the repair paint needs a key. Remove dust with a damp cloth.

2

Fill and seal if wood is damaged

Crushed corners and gouges: fill with two-part filler, sand flush, re-form the radius, seal the filler with thinned PVA or thinned paint and let it dry. Bare end grain: one thin brushed coat of paint as a sealer first.

3

Re-texture with the same paint

  • Use the same product, same colour, ideally the same batch kept from the original job. Keep 1 litre per cabinet set in a sealed tin at 15 to 25 °C for repairs.
  • Small chips: stipple with a stiff brush or a 50 to 100 mm texture mini-roller, two or three coats, each dry to the touch before the next. Feather each coat a little further into the surrounding texture.
  • Where the surrounding texture is already thick, use a short-pile roller so you do not build a lump.
  • Large areas or a whole face: sand the whole face with P80 and re-roll the complete panel. Only a complete panel will be invisible; a patch in the middle of a face will show in low light however well it is done.
4

Cure before use

24 hours before the cabinet is stacked or cased, 4 to 7 days before hard use. Expect a repair on an aged cabinet to differ slightly in colour, sheen and texture: faded or dirty old paint next to fresh paint. Clean the whole cabinet first so the difference is smallest.

Method B cabinets (2K PU textured)

  • Sand the damage and 30 mm around it with P120 to P180, feather, fill bare wood with 2K filler, spot-seal with 2K PU or epoxy primer, let it cure and sand.
  • Mix a small quantity of the same liner, tinted as the original, and spray with the Schutz gun through a card mask with a soft edge, or stipple with a brush for very small chips. Two light coats. Full isocyanate PPE applies even for small repairs that are sprayed.
  • Texture and sheen mismatch is normal on PU repairs. For a visible face, re-coat the whole face. Re-apply the matt aliphatic topcoat on outdoor cabinets.
  • Never touch up 2K PU with water-based texture paint: it will flake off within weeks.

Method C cabinets (polyurea)

  • Repair chips quickly: water that enters through a chip cannot dry out through the skin, and the plywood will swell and delaminate under an intact-looking finish.
  • Cut back any loose or lifted skin to firmly bonded material with a sharp knife. Dry the wood (fan, days if needed). Fill deep damage with 2K epoxy filler.
  • Field patch: a brushable or cartridge two-component polyurea or 2K PU repair compound from the polyurea maker, worked into the hole slightly proud, textured with a stipple brush before it gels, then the matt aliphatic topcoat in RAL 7016. The patch will be visible.
  • Invisible repair means a return to the applicator for a re-spray of the face or the whole cabinet. EAW states plainly that its textured roadcoat "will make it difficult to blend touch-up paint". Plan for this when choosing Method C.
Tip

Whatever the method, keep a repair kit per cabinet set: 1 litre of the paint (or a small liner kit and hardener with a long shelf life), the RAL card, a 100 mm texture roller, a stipple brush, P80 and P120 paper, filler, thinned PVA, and the job sheet with batch numbers.

12.2 The Indonesia recipe for dark grey matte

Every dedicated local texture paint is black. Dark grey therefore has to come from a topcoat over a textured black base (or from a tinted 2K system). Three routes, in order of preference for an ordinary shop:

Route 1 (default): local black texture base + tinted matte water-based acrylic topcoat in RAL 7016

  1. Prepare and texture exactly as Section 4 (Steps 1 to 13) using Vircan, Caldera or Prismatik class paint as the texture base: bare sanded ply, filler sealed, no primer, undiluted (the local guidance says no thinning), kamprot roller or hopper gun, three coats plus the edge coat. Treat the black base as the protective, scratch-hiding layer.
  2. Cure the base at least 48 hours in a dry room (7 days is better) before topcoating. Wipe clean, scuff lightly with a grey non-woven pad to dull any sheen, remove dust with a damp cloth.
  3. Topcoat with a tinted exterior-grade 100 % acrylic water-based paint, RAL 7016, matt ("doff"), mixed at a Jotun, Nippon or Dulux tinting centre (12.4). Two coats by kamprot roller or by the same texture gun at a finer setting, each dry to the touch before the next. The topcoat follows the texture and makes it slightly softer; a heavier second coat gives a finer look, a lighter one keeps more texture. Set this on the test panel.
  4. Cure 48 hours before stacking, 7 to 14 days before touring (water-based acrylics in this climate reach full hardness slowly; general guidance says 14 to 30 days).
Adhesion caveat and mandatory test panel

This route stacks two different water-based acrylics of unknown formulation. Acrylic on acrylic normally bonds well, but it is not guaranteed. Make a test panel through all steps, cure 7 days, and pass the cross-hatch (4B or better), the 24-hour wet soak and the scuff test (Section 8) before painting any cabinet. Also note that a wall-paint topcoat is softer than a cabinet texture paint: hard scuffs will show the black base through the grey. That is cosmetic, the black base is still protecting the wood, and the fix is a quick re-roll of the topcoat (Section 9). Alternatives to try on panels: ask the Vircan or Caldera seller for a custom grey batch (small producers, unverified), or tint a white texture base such as Propan DTX white; neither has been confirmed to work.

Route 2 (tougher): sealed, textured base + 2K PU topcoat tinted to RAL 7016 matt

  1. Seal the bare ply with a 2K epoxy primer (Nippon nax 2200 or similar) or a 2K PU sealer (Propan PUL-91 clear), cured and sanded P180 to P240, as in Section 5, Step 2. For previously painted and fiberglass cabinets, prepare and prime as Section 5A.
  2. Build the texture in the 2K system itself. Preferred: apply the first coat of the 2K topcoat thick through a Schutz gun, hopper gun or kamprot roller to create the texture, then one or two normal coats for colour and sheen. A textured 2K primer coat (the sealer sprayed heavy at low air pressure) is the other option. Set texture on the panel first.
  3. Topcoat product, two choices:
    (a) Propan DURATEX 2K PU DRX 890 WB: water-based 2K acrylic PU, low sheen, tinted per Propan colour card at a Propan Multicolor Center. Two to three coats. Lower solvent load and inherently low sheen make it the easier choice for a matte result.
    (b) Jotun Hardtop XP or AX tinted to RAL 7016 at a Jotun MCI dealer. Both are glossy by default: ask for the matt variant (Hardtop Eco Matt or XPL; Indonesian availability unconfirmed) or a dealer-approved matting additive, and prove the sheen on the panel (target 10 GU or less at 60°, or visually dead matt next to the RAL card). Pot life 1.5 h at 23 °C, shorter in heat.
  4. Alternative base if you must use the local black water-based texture under the 2K: cure it at least 7 days, scuff, and prove adhesion on a panel first. A hard 2K film over a soft 1K base is the combination that fails most often; the test panel decides.
  5. Cure per the TDS; 24 h before handling, 48 h before stacking, 7 days before touring; keep RH below 85 % until walk-on dry or the 2K will blush and lose gloss uniformity.
Isocyanate PPE

Every product in this route, including the water-based DRX 890 WB, cures with a polyisocyanate hardener. Isocyanate 2K is sold freely in Indonesia with no purchase restriction, so the control is entirely on the shop: supplied-air respirator for spraying (3M Versaflo or airline hood through a 3M distributor; marketplace half masks are for mixing and roller work only), enclosed and extracted spray area, run the extraction after spraying, nobody else in the room, nitrile gloves, coveralls. Section 5 applies in full.

Route 3 (optional): polyurea applicator

Use only if a contractor can prove, with a sprayed sample on your plywood offcut: a sealer or primer system for plywood, 1 to 1.5 mm in two passes, an even medium texture, and a pigmented aliphatic or water-based grey topcoat in RAL 7016 matt that passes the cross-hatch test. The contractors found are roof and tank waterproofers; none advertises small-object work, coloured topcoats or UV-stable systems, and no price per m² could be verified. Expect a minimum charge and the weight penalty of Section 3. Specify the job as in Section 6.

BAppendix B: Glossary

1K / 2K
One-component paint (dries from the tin) versus two-component paint (base plus hardener, cures by chemical reaction, has a pot life).
Acrylic dispersion (water-based texture paint)
Acrylic resin particles in water; the film forms as the water leaves and the particles fuse. Low hazard, UV stable, repairable, softer than 2K systems.
Aliphatic / aromatic
Two families of isocyanate. Aromatic (MDI, TDI) is cheap and fast but fades and chalks in sunlight; aliphatic (HDI, IPDI) is light-stable. Bed-liners are mostly aromatic; UV topcoats are aliphatic.
Chalking
Breakdown of the paint surface under UV into a loose, pale powder. Very visible on grey.
Cross-hatch (ASTM D3359 / ISO 2409)
Adhesion test: a lattice is cut through the coating, tape is applied and pulled off, and the amount of coating removed is graded (5B best to 0B worst).
D4 glue (EN 204)
Highest water-resistance class for wood adhesives; used in exterior and marine plywood specified by touring brands.
DFT
Dry film thickness, in micrometres (µm) or mils (1 mil = 25.4 µm). Texture paint about 120 µm; polyurea 1000 to 1500 µm.
Dust-dry / handle / through-dry
Stages of drying: dust no longer sticks; the part can be carried; the film has reached its final hardness. Only through-dry paint should go on the road.
GU (gloss units)
Reflectance measured by a gloss meter, usually at 60 degrees (ISO 2813). Matt is roughly 10 GU or less; satin 20 to 35; gloss above 70.
Honeycomb roller
Open-cell foam roller with a coarse honeycomb structure that leaves a spatter texture. The standard roller for speaker cabinet paint.
Hopper gun
Gravity-fed texture gun with a large (4 to 8 mm) nozzle, used for drywall texture and for spray-grade cabinet paint.
IP rating (IEC 60529)
Ingress protection code; IP54/55 is dust-protected and resistant to splashing or jetted water. It says nothing about scratch resistance.
Isocyanate
Reactive component of polyurethane and polyurea hardeners. Potent respiratory sensitiser; spraying requires supplied-air respiratory protection and, in the EU, documented training (REACH Regulation 2020/1149).
MDF
Medium-density fibreboard. Has a dense, waxy surface skin that must be sanded off before painting; edges are very absorbent.
PCP
"Polyurea Cabinet Protection", d&b audiotechnik's name for its sprayed polyurea finish; also used by KV2.
Plural-component rig
Heated, high-pressure pump that meters two components and mixes them in the gun. Required for polyurea. Not shop equipment.
Polyurea
Elastomer from an isocyanate and an amine; gels in seconds, forms a thick rubbery skin. The toughest cabinet finish in current use.
Polyurethane (PU)
Polymer from an isocyanate and a polyol; slower cure; used as 2K textured paint, sealers and topcoats. Note that Funktion-One's "PU coating" is described in its own brochure as polyurea.
Pot life
Time after mixing a 2K product during which it can still be sprayed. Shorter in heat.
Pressure pot / pressure-cup gun
Spray equipment that pushes thick paint to the gun under air pressure instead of relying on gravity or suction. Needed for viscous texture paint.
RAL
European colour standard. RAL 7016 Anthracite Grey, 7021 Black Grey, 7024 Graphite Grey, 9005 Jet Black.
Schutz gun
Underbody-coating gun with a screw-on 1 litre bottle and a large nozzle; the standard gun for bed-liner products and usable for texture paint.
SDS / TDS
Safety data sheet (hazards, PPE) and technical data sheet (application, drying, thickness). The TDS of the product you buy overrides this guide.
Sealer coat
First thin coat that soaks into wood or filler to even out absorption, so later coats bond and build evenly.
Spatter (texture) coat
A spray pass with the gun set to throw coarse droplets, giving the dimpled "orange peel" surface.
VOC
Volatile organic compounds. Water-based texture paints are low-VOC (not zero: Warnex contains 6 to 9 % glycol ethers) and need ventilation when sprayed.
